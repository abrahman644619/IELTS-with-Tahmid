The Recycling Debate

Meherin had spent three years studying environmental science, but it was only during this term break that her research finally felt personal. For her final-year project, a community waste audit had been assigned to her, and she had chosen the market near her family's home in Sylhet as her site — mostly because her uncle, Momtaz Mia, ran a small grocery shop there, and she assumed his cooperation could be taken for granted.

She was wrong.

"You want me to keep three separate bins?" Momtaz Mia asked, wiping his hands on his lungi. "One for plastic, one for paper, one for... what did you call it? Organic waste?" He laughed, not unkindly. "Meherin, this shop has been run by our family for twenty years. Customers throw their wrappers wherever there's space. That habit will not be changed by three bins."

"It won't be changed overnight," Meherin admitted, "but habits are shaped by systems, Uncle. If nothing is provided, nothing will be sorted. If the option is given, at least some customers will use it."

Momtaz Mia was not convinced. "And who collects it afterwards? The municipal truck comes once a week, and everything — plastic, paper, vegetable peels — is thrown into the same container by the same workers. Your three bins will be emptied into one van. What was the point of the separation?"

This was, Meherin knew, a fair criticism — one that had already been raised by her supervisor, in fact. The absence of a citywide segregation system was a real limitation, and no amount of enthusiasm on her part could be substituted for infrastructure that did not yet exist. But she had also read enough case studies to know that behavioural change was rarely initiated by governments alone; it was often driven, at least at first, by small, visible, local efforts.

"You're right that the system isn't ready," she said. "But the data has to come from somewhere. If a pilot isn't tried by someone, the argument for a proper system will never be made convincingly to the city corporation. I'm not asking you to solve waste management in Sylhet. I'm asking for six weeks and three bins."

Her uncle folded his arms. Behind him, a customer was being handed change, and a plastic packet of biscuits was being dropped, without a second thought, straight onto the floor beside the counter. Meherin gestured toward it. "That's what I mean," she said quietly.

Momtaz Mia was silent for a moment. Then he said, "Six weeks. And if my sales are affected — if customers are annoyed by being asked to sort their own rubbish — the bins are removed, and we don't discuss it again."

It was not the enthusiastic agreement Meherin had hoped for, but it was, she reminded herself, still an agreement. Over the following days, three labelled bins were placed near the shop entrance, a small hand-painted sign was put up by her cousin, and a simple log was kept of what was discarded and where.

The results, when they came, were not dramatic. Most customers ignored the bins entirely. A few, mostly younger ones, made a visible effort to use them correctly. Contamination — plastic wrappers found in the "organic" bin, vegetable peels found in the "paper" bin — was common. Whether this counted as failure or as a modest, realistic beginning was, Meherin realised, a matter of perspective rather than fact.

On the final evening of her project, she sat with her uncle as he closed the shop. "So," he said, "was I right?"

"Partly," Meherin said. "The habits weren't changed as fast as I'd hoped. But something was noticed by people who never would have noticed it before. I don't know if that's enough. Maybe it isn't."

Momtaz Mia considered this for a long moment. "Keep the bins," he said finally. "Not because I believe in your three-bin theory. Because you asked properly, and you didn't pretend it was simple." He shrugged. "That's something, isn't it?"

Meherin wasn't sure if it was a victory. But she wrote it down anyway.

SECTION 8 | CHARACTER REFLECTION | Character Corner

Instructions: These questions have no single correct answer. Discuss with a partner if possible, then write your own considered response.

1. Momtaz Mia agreed to the trial not because he was convinced by the argument, but because Meherin "asked properly" and "didn't pretend it was simple." What does this suggest about how change is actually negotiated between generations, as opposed to how it is argued for in theory?

2. To what extent should young people push their elders toward new practices (environmental or otherwise), and to what extent should they accept established, existing ways of doing things?

3. The story ends without a clear resolution — the bins remain, but neither character is fully proven right. Why might a story without a tidy conclusion be more honest than one with a clear moral lesson?

4. Is a "modest, realistic beginning" enough to justify the effort involved, or is meaningful change only meaningful when it happens at scale?
