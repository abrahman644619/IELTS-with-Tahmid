The Commute

Farzana, who has worked at a private bank in Motijheel for the past four years, spends nearly three hours every day travelling to and from her office in Mirpur — a commute that, under normal circumstances, should take no more than forty minutes. The single flyover near her route had long ago become the city's most notorious bottleneck, and some mornings the congestion dissolved into complete gridlock before she had even left her own neighbourhood. Having left home before seven for as long as she can remember, she had grown used to mornings that began in darkness and evenings that ended the same way.

On a rare free Saturday, she noticed a small notice pinned to the gate of the local ward office, announcing a public consultation on a proposed metro rail corridor — a route that, according to the notice, would eventually connect several densely populated areas to the existing network. Curious, and frankly a little unconvinced, she decided to attend.

The meeting was led by Architect Kamal Reza, an urban planner who had spent nearly a decade working on Dhaka's transport projects. Having presented the proposed route on a large printed map, he opened the floor to questions.

Farzana raised her hand. "The map you've shown us doesn't include Mirpur-2, which is where thousands of commuters like me actually live. Why does this corridor skip the area that arguably needs it most?"

Kamal Reza, who had clearly answered variations of this question many times before, nodded patiently. "That's a fair question, and I won't pretend there's a simple answer. The corridor was prioritised using ridership data collected over three years — data that showed higher potential passenger volume along the route we've chosen. Mirpur-2, unfortunately, didn't score as highly in that particular model."

"So residents whose daily commute is already unbearable," Farzana replied, "are being asked to wait for a phase that might not exist for another decade — because a model, built from data none of us ever saw, decided we mattered less?"

A murmur passed through the room; several people nodded, clearly feeling as marginalised by the process as she did. Kamal Reza paused before answering. "I understand why that sounds unfair. A city that tried to fund every route at once would build nothing well. Budgets have to be allocated somewhere first, sometimes at the expense of neighbourhoods like your own. The extension approved for this phase is the one projected to relieve congestion for the largest number of people, not necessarily the people facing the worst individual commutes today."

"That's a difference that matters enormously if you're one of the people not being helped," Farzana said quietly.

"It does," he admitted. "I'm not going to stand here and tell you this plan is equitable for everyone, because it isn't, not yet. What I can tell you is that the data used to exclude Mirpur-2 this year is also the data that will justify including it in the next phase — assuming the growth pattern continues, and assuming next year's budget is actually approved. There is, in fact, a second corridor, still only in the pipeline, that would eventually reach your area as well."

"And if the budget isn't approved?" someone else asked.

"Then we go back to the drawing board on prioritisation, and areas like Mirpur-2 keep waiting," Kamal Reza said. "I won't pretend that's a satisfying answer. It's simply the honest one."

After the meeting ended, Farzana remained for a while, looking again at the printed map pinned to the board. A young volunteer, noticing her still standing there, offered to note her comments for the official record. Farzana wrote a short paragraph — measured, unlike the frustration she had walked in with — asking that Mirpur-2's data be weighed up again before the next phase was finalised.

Walking home, she thought about what Kamal Reza had said. A transport system designed to help the most people possible would, by definition, leave some people waiting, even when a fully equitable plan felt more feasible in theory than viable within an actual limited budget. That didn't make her three-hour commute any shorter. But she found, somewhat unwillingly, that she no longer knew whether to be angry at the planners or simply at the scale of the problem itself.

She still had no faster way home that evening. But she had, for the first time, a clearer picture of why.

D. Sentence Transformation

Instructions: Combine each pair of sentences as directed, using the required grammar structure.

1. Original: Farzana works at a bank. She commutes for three hours a day.

Combine using a non-defining relative clause:

Farzana, , commutes for three hours a day.

2. Original: Kamal Reza presented the map. Then he opened the floor to questions.

Combine using a perfect participle clause (Having...):

, he opened the floor to questions.

3. Original: The volunteer noticed Farzana standing there. He offered to help.

Combine using a present participle clause:

The volunteer, , offered to help.

4. Original: Planners used the data to exclude Mirpur-2. The same data will justify including it later.

Combine using a reduced (contact) relative clause:

The data is the same data that will justify including it later.

SECTION 8 | CHARACTER REFLECTION | Character Corner

Instructions: These questions have no single correct answer. Discuss with a partner if possible, then write your own considered response.

1. Kamal Reza says, "I'm not going to stand here and tell you this plan is equitable for everyone, because it isn't, not yet." What does his willingness to admit this suggest about honest leadership, compared to leadership that simply reassures people?

2. Farzana ends the story with a clearer understanding of the system, but no shorter commute. Is understanding why something is unfair a meaningful outcome on its own, even without a solution?

3. To what extent should decisions that affect many people be based purely on data (like ridership numbers), rather than on which communities are struggling the most right now?

4. Is it possible to design any system — transport, education, healthcare — that is fair to absolutely everyone at the same time? What does this story suggest about that question?

5. Farzana chooses to submit a written comment rather than simply remaining angry. What does this suggest about the difference between venting frustration and engaging constructively with a system you disagree with?
