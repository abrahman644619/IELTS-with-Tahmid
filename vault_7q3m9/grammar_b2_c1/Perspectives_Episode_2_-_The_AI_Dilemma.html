The AI Dilemma

Tanvir had submitted the report three days early — an unusual habit for him — and for two of those days, he felt genuinely proud of it. The assignment, a comparative analysis of network security protocols, was for his favourite course in the CSE department, and he had used an AI writing assistant to help him organise his scattered notes into something that read, he thought, like a proper research paper rather than a rushed submission.

He had not mentioned this to anyone. Using AI felt less like cheating and more like working with a very capable editor — at least, that was what he told himself when he clicked "submit."

On Thursday afternoon, an email arrived: Dr. Farzana Elahi wanted to see him before his next class.

His friend Naeem was unhelpful in the way that only a good friend can be. "She must have noticed something," he said, as they walked toward the department office. "Your last two assignments read completely differently from this one. Did you really think nobody would compare them?"

"I didn't think about it that carefully," Tanvir admitted.

"Well, you should have," Naeem said. "You can't have expected a professor who reads forty reports a semester not to notice a sudden jump in vocabulary. She might have let a small change go unnoticed. A jump like this one, though — she must have flagged it immediately."

Dr. Elahi's office was small and lined with journals. She did not begin with an accusation. "Tanvir," she said, "I'd like you to explain, in your own words, how the ideas in section three came together. Just talk me through your thinking."

Tanvir hesitated. When she asked directly whether he had understood the encryption method fully before submitting it, he first denied that there was any real gap in his understanding. Then, under her calm, patient silence, he corrected himself. He admitted that he had used an AI tool to help with the report. He insisted, however, that he had used it only to organise ideas he already had and to phrase certain sections more clearly than he could have done on his own.

Dr. Elahi listened without interrupting. When he finished, she said that intention did not remove the need for honesty. She explained that the problem was not necessarily the tool itself; it was that he had presented assisted work as entirely his own, without giving her — or himself — an accurate picture of what he actually understood.

"Can you explain the encryption method in section three to me now, without the report in front of you?" she asked.

Tanvir tried. He got through the first half confidently, then stumbled. He admitted that the tool had explained that part better than he could have explained it himself, and that he had not fully checked whether he understood it before submitting the analysis as his own.

"That," Dr. Elahi said, "is exactly what I wanted you to notice. Not that you asked for help structuring your ideas — every writer does that in some form. The problem is that a gap in your own understanding was hidden behind confident-sounding sentences that weren't really yours."

She suggested that he rewrite section three independently, using only his own words, and that in future assignments, he disclose clearly which parts of his work had been shaped by AI tools and how. Tanvir agreed, somewhat relieved that the conversation had not ended more severely.

Walking out, he told Naeem what had happened. Naeem asked whether Tanvir now thought using AI for coursework was simply wrong. Tanvir considered this for a long moment. "I don't think it's that simple," he said. "If I'd used it and still understood everything, and just said so honestly, I don't think it would have been a problem. The tool isn't really the issue. What I hid was."

Naeem wasn't fully convinced. "Half the department uses these tools quietly," he said. "If everyone declared it, would professors even have time to check everything properly? Isn't the whole policy sort of built on people not being fully honest about it?"

Tanvir didn't have an answer to that. He only knew that, for his own report, hiding the gap had cost him more than declaring it honestly would have.

SECTION 8 | CHARACTER REFLECTION | Character Corner

Instructions: These questions have no single correct answer. Discuss with a partner if possible, then write your own considered response.

1. Tanvir concludes, "The tool isn't really the issue. What I hid was." To what extent do you agree that honesty about a process matters more than the process itself?

2. Naeem suggests that the whole disclosure policy might only function because most students do not fully comply with it. What does this reveal about rules that depend on individual honesty rather than enforcement?

3. Is there a meaningful difference between using a tool to organise understanding you already have, and using a tool to fill a gap in understanding you do not yet have? Where would you personally draw the line?

4. Dr. Elahi chose to ask Tanvir to explain the material aloud rather than accuse him directly. What does this approach suggest about how trust can be rebuilt after a mistake, rather than simply punished?
